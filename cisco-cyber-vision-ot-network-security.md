# Cisco Cyber Vision License: What’s Really Happening Inside Your Industrial Network?

Imagine working in a factory where hundreds of devices communicate every day. Most of them do exactly what they are supposed to do. But what if one machine suddenly starts talking to a device it has never connected to before? Would anyone notice?

In an office network, unusual activity might be easier to investigate. Industrial environments are different. Some machines have been running for years, production cannot simply stop for a security check, and even a small change in network traffic can have consequences.

That makes visibility a real challenge for OT security teams.

[Cisco Cyber Vision](https://golicense.net/product-category/cisco-license/security/cyber-vision/) License is designed to help organizations understand what is happening inside their industrial networks. 

It identifies connected devices, shows how they communicate, and helps security teams spot vulnerabilities and unusual activity. 

Instead of seeing a network as a list of IP addresses, teams can get a better understanding of the equipment behind those connections.

For example, a security team might need to know which PLCs are communicating with an engineering workstation, whether a device has known vulnerabilities, or why an industrial controller has started exchanging unexpected traffic. These details can make a big difference when investigating a potential security issue.

Cyber Vision collects information from industrial network traffic through supported sensors and brings it together in Cyber Vision Center. 

Because it supports passive monitoring, organizations can gain useful insight without relying on intrusive scans of sensitive production equipment.

The licensing options are fairly straightforward.

Essentials focuses on asset discovery, communication visibility, and vulnerability management. Advantage adds capabilities such as device risk scoring, behavioral monitoring, and intrusion detection.

Licensing is generally based on the number of monitored endpoints, with subscription terms of one, three, five, or seven years. The right option depends on the size of the environment, the security features required, and the existing industrial infrastructure.

Of course, finding unusual activity is only part of the job.

Someone still needs to investigate the alert, understand what changed, and decide what to do without disrupting production.

Because in industrial security, the biggest risk isn't always a device you don't know exists. Sometimes, it's a device you know very well that suddenly starts behaving differently.
